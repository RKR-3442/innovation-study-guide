⚖️ Intellectual Property Rights (IPR)

🔥 VERY IMPORTANT ⭐ MEMORIZE DURATIONS 📌 COMMON EXAM TRAP
IPR Types = PTCD (Patent / Trade Secret / Copyright / Design) — remember the durations and criteria for each

NUI Test — Patent Requirements

Mnemonic: "NUI" — 3 requirements to get a patent

N-U-I
  • Novelty: Must be new — not previously disclosed anywhere in the world (not in prior art)
  • Useful: Must have industrial application — can be used or made in industry
  • Inventive step: Non-obvious — would not be obvious to a "person skilled in the art"
Copyright — Key Points
  • Automatic protection: No registration needed — copyright arises automatically when work is created
  • Idea-Expression Dichotomy: Copyright protects the EXPRESSION of an idea (the specific words, code, design) — NOT the idea itself. Anyone can use the same idea, just not the exact expression.
  • Example: You can write a book about a wizard school — but you cannot copy J.K. Rowling's exact text.

UNDERSTAND: IPR Trade-Off

Core Tension: Stronger IPR = More monopoly power for innovators (incentive to invest in R&D) BUT less knowledge diffusion for society (others can't use/build on the knowledge).

Formula: Optimal IPR policy balances innovation incentive vs. knowledge access for follow-on innovation.
IBM Patent Portfolio Example
  • IBM files ~9,000 patents per year — largest US patent holder for 28+ consecutive years
  • Revenue from patent licensing: over $1 billion annually
  • Strategy: patents are both defensive (protection) and offensive (licensing revenue stream)
  • Cross-licensing: IBM licenses patents to other companies in exchange for access to their patents

COMMON EXAM TRAP: Copyright is AUTOMATIC — no registration needed. Students often confuse copyright (automatic) with patents (must be applied for and examined). Also: Copyright protects EXPRESSION not ideas — the idea-expression dichotomy is frequently tested.

Most Likely Exam Questions

Explain the four types of IPR and their role in protecting innovation▼
Full Question (15 marks): A technology startup has developed a new AI-powered medical diagnostic device. The device includes a novel algorithm, a distinctive physical design, and a unique brand name. The software code was written by the founders. Identify and explain the most appropriate IPR protection for each element. Justify your recommendations using relevant criteria.

Model Answer (15 marks)

[1. Overview of IPR Types - 3 marks]

IPR (Intellectual Property Rights) are legal mechanisms that grant innovators exclusive rights to their creations, enabling recovery of R&D investment while preventing unauthorized use. Four main types: Patent, Trade Secret, Copyright, and Design Rights (PTCD).

[2. Novel Algorithm — Patent or Trade Secret Analysis - 4 marks]

Option A: Patent the algorithm if it meets NUI criteria:

  • Novelty: Algorithm not previously disclosed (check prior art/patent databases)
  • Useful: Has industrial/medical application ✓
  • Inventive step: Non-obvious improvement over existing diagnostic methods

Patent gives 20-year monopoly with full disclosure. Others can see it but cannot use it without license.

Option B: Trade Secret if reverse-engineering is difficult and secrecy is maintainable. Unlimited duration but no protection against independent discovery or reverse engineering.

Recommendation: If algorithm is truly novel and inventive, patent is preferable for medical sector where regulatory approval requires disclosure anyway.

[3. Distinctive Physical Design — Design Right - 3 marks]

The physical appearance of the device = Design Right protection.

  • Duration: 15 years
  • Requirement: New and individual character
  • Protects aesthetic appearance: shape, configuration, patterns, colors

Example: Apple successfully used design rights to block Samsung's Galaxy Tab in EU (2012) for resembling iPad design.

[4. Unique Brand Name — Trademark - 2 marks]

Brand name + logo = Trademark

  • Duration: Indefinitely renewable (10-year periods)
  • Protects brand identity and prevents consumer confusion
  • Must be registered in each jurisdiction of use

[5. Software Code Written by Founders — Copyright - 3 marks]

Software code = Copyright protection

  • Automatic: No registration needed — copyright arises immediately when code is written
  • Duration: 70 years after author's death
  • Idea-expression dichotomy: Protects the specific code (expression) but NOT the underlying algorithm concept (idea). This is why patent protection is also needed for the algorithm.

Conclusion: The startup should use a layered IPR strategy: Patent (algorithm) + Design Right (device appearance) + Trademark (brand name) + Copyright (software code). This combination maximizes protection across all valuable assets.

Critically analyze the IPR trade-off between innovation incentive and knowledge diffusion▼
Full Question (12 marks): "Intellectual Property Rights are a necessary evil — they incentivize innovation but restrict the free flow of knowledge that also drives innovation." Critically analyze this statement, using examples from the pharmaceutical industry and the technology sector.

Model Answer (12 marks)

[1. The Core Trade-Off - 2 marks]

IPR systems create a fundamental tension: Innovation needs both (a) incentives to invest in R&D and (b) access to existing knowledge to build upon. Strong IPR maximizes (a) but reduces (b). Weak IPR maximizes (b) but reduces (a) — reducing investment in costly innovation.

[2. Argument FOR strong IPR — Incentive View - 4 marks]

Pharmaceutical example: Developing a new drug costs $1-2 billion and takes 10-15 years. Without patent protection (20 years), generic manufacturers could immediately copy the drug at minimal cost. No company would invest $1B+ if anyone could copy for $50M. Therefore, patents are essential to justify pharmaceutical R&D investment.

IBM example: IBM invests $6B+ annually in R&D. Their ~9,000 patents/year generate $1B+ in licensing revenue, which funds future R&D. Without IPR, this innovation engine would collapse.

Academic evidence: Countries with stronger IPR protection generally attract more foreign direct investment in R&D-intensive industries (OECD studies).

[3. Argument AGAINST strong IPR — Diffusion View - 4 marks]

Pharmaceutical example: HIV drugs patented at $15,000/year per patient. Sub-Saharan Africa could not afford treatment. Millions died. Patent protection blocked access to life-saving medicine. The Doha Declaration (2001) allowed developing countries to issue compulsory licenses — acknowledging IPR can override public health.

Technology sector: The "patent thicket" problem — dense networks of overlapping patents that block follow-on innovation. Smartphone patents: Apple vs Samsung involved over 300 patents. Small startups cannot navigate patent thickets, reducing innovation dynamism.

Open source movement: Linux, Wikipedia, open-source AI models demonstrate that innovation flourishes without IPR when knowledge is freely shared. Some innovations occur faster WITHOUT patents.

[4. Optimal Balance - 2 marks]

Most economists favor calibrated IPR: duration and breadth matched to actual R&D costs and competitive dynamics. Short monopoly = less incentive. Too long = knowledge locked away too long. Pharmaceutical patents (20 years) may be appropriate; software patents (also 20 years) likely too long given rapid technological change.

Conclusion: IPR is neither purely "necessary" nor purely "evil" — it is a policy instrument that must be calibrated. The current system overprotects in some areas (software, pharma duration) and underprotects in others (traditional knowledge, geographical indications). Reform requires industry-specific analysis rather than one-size-fits-all rules.

Common Confusion: Patent vs Copyright

Patent

Must APPLY for and pass examination. Protects INVENTIONS (technical solutions). 20-year duration.

Copyright

AUTOMATIC — no registration. Protects CREATIVE WORKS (expression, not ideas). 70 years after death.

Key exam trap: Software gets BOTH — copyright protects the code, patent protects the underlying algorithm if it meets NUI criteria.
