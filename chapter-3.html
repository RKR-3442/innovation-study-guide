📈 Innovation Theories

🔥 VERY IMPORTANT ⭐ SPAD MNEMONIC 🧠 4 MAJOR THEORIES
Innovation Theories = SPAD: S-curve + Punctuated Equilibrium + Absorptive Capacity + Dominant Design

MEMORIZE: 4 Innovation Theories — SPAD

Mnemonic: "SPAD" — 4 core theories

S-P-A-D
  • S = S-Curve of Technology
  • P = Punctuated Equilibrium
  • A = Absorptive Capacity
  • D = Dominant Design
Theory 1: S-Curve — 3 Phases
Pattern = slow → fast → slow (Emergence → Growth → Maturity)
PhaseWhat HappensInnovation TypeExample
1. Emergence (bottom of S)Slow growth, high uncertainty, few adopters, high costRadical / breakthrough innovationsEarly smartphones (2000-2007)
2. Growth (steep middle)Rapid adoption, standards emerge, cost drops, scaleIncremental + architectural innovationsSmartphone explosion (2007-2015)
3. Maturity (top flat)Saturation, incremental improvements, commoditizationProcess innovations, cost reductionSmartphone market today
Theory 2: Punctuated Equilibrium
Pattern = stable → disruption → new stable
  • Long periods of relative stability with incremental improvement (equilibrium)
  • "Punctuated" by sudden radical technological discontinuities (punctuations)
  • After disruption: new equilibrium established around new technology
Kodak Example: Film photography was stable for 100 years (incremental improvement in film quality, camera design). Digital photography = punctuation event (technological discontinuity). Kodak failed to transition to new equilibrium despite INVENTING digital camera (1975). Result: bankruptcy 2012.
Theory 3: Dominant Design — F-T-S

Mnemonic: "FTS" — 3 phases of dominant design

F-T-S
  • Fluid phase: Many competing designs, no standard, high experimentation. Customer preferences unclear.
  • Transitional phase: One design begins to emerge as preferred. Competitors adopt or modify it.
  • Specific phase: One dominant design established. Firms compete on price/process, not product variety.

iPhone Example (Dominant Design in Smartphones):

  • Fluid (2000-2007): Nokia QWERTY, Blackberry keyboard, Motorola flip, Windows Mobile — many competing designs
  • Transitional (2007-2010): iPhone touchscreen wins user preference. Android adopts same paradigm.
  • Specific (2010+): Full-touchscreen slab = dominant design. All smartphones converge. QWERTY keyboards disappear.
Theory 4: Absorptive Capacity — R-A-A

Mnemonic: "RAA" — Cohen & Levinthal

R-A-A
  • Recognize: Identify valuable external knowledge (requires prior related knowledge)
  • Assimilate: Integrate external knowledge into existing knowledge base
  • Apply: Use assimilated knowledge for commercial innovation
Key Insight: "Prior knowledge is required to absorb new knowledge." You need existing expertise to recognize, understand, and apply external knowledge. This is why firms invest in basic R&D even when they license technology — it builds absorptive capacity.
Kondratiev Waves (Long Economic Cycles)
  • Long-wave economic cycles of ~50 years, driven by clusters of innovation
  • Wave 1: Industrial Revolution (textiles, iron)
  • Wave 2: Railways, steam power
  • Wave 3: Electricity, chemicals, steel
  • Wave 4: Automotive, oil, aerospace
  • Wave 5: ICT, internet, software
  • Wave 6 (emerging): AI, biotech, green energy?

UNDERSTAND: How Theories Connect

S-Curve + Punctuated Equilibrium: When a technology reaches S-curve maturity, it becomes vulnerable to punctuation (radical disruption). The new technology starts a new S-curve. This is why Kodak failed — they were at the top of the film photography S-curve when digital punctuated the equilibrium.
Dominant Design + S-Curve: Dominant design typically emerges during the Growth phase of the S-curve. Once dominant design is established, innovation transitions from product to process innovation.
Absorptive Capacity + All Theories: Firms with high absorptive capacity can recognize when punctuation is coming, adopt new dominant designs faster, and navigate S-curve transitions more successfully.
S-curve questions: ALWAYS name all 3 phases and describe what happens in each. Punctuated equilibrium questions: ALWAYS use Kodak example. Dominant Design questions: iPhone example is perfect. Absorptive capacity questions: explain why PRIOR knowledge matters.

Most Likely Exam Questions

Explain the S-curve of technological development and its strategic implications▼
Full Question (15 marks): The electric vehicle (EV) market is currently experiencing rapid growth globally. Using the S-curve of technological development, analyze the current stage of EV technology and predict the strategic challenges that EV manufacturers will face as the technology matures. Use relevant examples to support your analysis.

Model Answer (15 marks)

[1. S-Curve Framework — 3 phases - 6 marks]

The S-curve (technology lifecycle) maps technology performance against cumulative investment/time, producing an S-shaped adoption pattern across three distinct phases:

Phase 1 — Emergence (slow growth): Technology is new, uncertain, expensive. Few adopters. High R&D costs per unit improvement. Problems not yet solved. Market unclear. Example: EV in 2000s — Tesla Roadster ($100K+), limited range, no charging infrastructure.

Phase 2 — Growth (rapid adoption): Technology improves rapidly. Costs fall dramatically. Standards emerge. Mass adoption begins. Investment flows in. Example: EVs 2015-present — Tesla Model 3 ($35K), 300+ mile range, global charging networks, major incumbents (GM, Ford, VW) switching to EVs.

Phase 3 — Maturity (saturation): Performance plateaus. Incremental improvements only. Competition on price/features rather than fundamental technology. Consolidation occurs. Example: Future EVs — battery performance approaches physical limits, competition on manufacturing efficiency, software, price.

[2. Current Stage of EV Technology - 3 marks]

EVs are currently in the Growth phase (steep part of S-curve):

  • Evidence: Global EV sales grew from 2M (2018) to 14M (2023) — 600% growth
  • Battery costs fell from $1,200/kWh (2010) to $130/kWh (2023)
  • Dominant design emerging: battery electric vehicle (BEV) over hydrogen fuel cell
  • Major incumbents committing to full EV transition (Ford 2035, GM 2035, VW 2030)

[3. Strategic Challenges as Technology Matures - 4 marks]

Challenge 1 — Dominant Design Competition: As growth phase ends, dominant design will crystallize. Manufacturers who chose wrong battery chemistry, charging standard, or software architecture will be stranded.

Challenge 2 — Process Innovation Shift: Mature phase = compete on manufacturing efficiency, not product innovation. Requires different capabilities. Chinese manufacturers (BYD, CATL) already optimizing EV production costs aggressively.

Challenge 3 — Punctuated Equilibrium Risk: A new technology (solid-state batteries, hydrogen fuel cells) could "punctuate" the current EV equilibrium. Manufacturers must invest in absorptive capacity to recognize and transition quickly.

Challenge 4 — Consolidation: Mature markets consolidate. Currently 500+ EV startups globally — most will fail or be acquired. Only 5-10 global players likely to survive.

[4. Strategic Recommendations - 2 marks]

  • Invest in next S-curve early (solid-state batteries) to prepare for next punctuation
  • Build absorptive capacity through R&D partnerships with battery universities
  • Lock in dominant design position now through software ecosystem (OTA updates, energy management)

Conclusion: Understanding where a technology sits on its S-curve is strategically critical. EV manufacturers in the growth phase must simultaneously win the current curve while investing in the next one — the classic innovator's dilemma.

Analyze Kodak's failure using Punctuated Equilibrium theory▼
Full Question (12 marks): In 1975, Kodak engineer Steve Sasson invented the digital camera. Yet by 2012, Kodak had filed for bankruptcy. Using the theory of Punctuated Equilibrium, analyze why Kodak failed to survive the transition from film to digital photography. What lessons does this case provide for today's technology companies?

Model Answer (12 marks)

[1. Punctuated Equilibrium Theory - 3 marks]

Punctuated Equilibrium (Tushman & Anderson) proposes that industries evolve through long periods of incremental change (equilibrium), interrupted by sudden radical technological discontinuities (punctuations) that fundamentally alter the basis of competition. After a punctuation, a new equilibrium forms around the disruptive technology, often making previous competencies obsolete.

[2. Kodak's Equilibrium Phase - 3 marks]

Kodak dominated film photography for over 100 years:

  • 1890s-1990s: Incremental improvements (faster film, better colors, one-hour developing)
  • Peak market share: 90% of US film, 85% of cameras
  • Revenue: $16B at peak (1996)
  • Business model: "Razors and blades" — sell cameras cheap, profit from film & developing
  • Core competencies: chemical film technology, retail distribution, photolab infrastructure

[3. The Punctuation Event - 3 marks]

Digital photography = technological discontinuity that rendered film photography's core competencies obsolete:

  • Kodak engineer Steve Sasson invented first digital camera (1975) — Kodak shelved it to protect film revenue
  • 1990s: Digital cameras improve exponentially (Moore's Law)
  • 2000s: Camera phone adoption destroys dedicated camera market
  • 2010s: Smartphone cameras + Instagram = complete digital transition

The punctuation made Kodak's chemical knowledge, film manufacturing, photolab network all worthless — "competence-destroying" technological discontinuity.

[4. Why Kodak Failed to Adapt - 2 marks]

  • Innovator's dilemma: Digital cannibalized film profits. Kodak couldn't commit to digital while film still profitable.
  • Low absorptive capacity for digital: Kodak's chemical/physical expertise didn't transfer to digital/software. Couldn't assimilate digital knowledge.
  • Cognitive lock-in: Leadership saw Kodak as a "film company" not an "imaging company" — missed the broader opportunity

[5. Lessons for Today's Companies - 1 mark]

  • Monitor the next S-curve — invest in it before current curve matures
  • Build absorptive capacity in emerging technologies (hire digital talent early)
  • Reframe identity around customer need, not technology medium ("imaging" not "film")
Analyze the iPhone using Dominant Design theory▼
Full Question (10 marks): When Apple launched the iPhone in 2007, the mobile phone market offered consumers many different designs including QWERTY keyboards, stylus devices, and flip phones. Using the Dominant Design framework (F-T-S), analyze how the iPhone established dominant design in the smartphone industry and the implications for firms that failed to adopt it.

Model Answer (10 marks)

[1. Dominant Design Theory — FTS Framework - 3 marks]

Dominant Design theory (Utterback & Abernathy) proposes that technology industries evolve through three phases: Fluid (F) — many competing designs; Transitional (T) — convergence begins; Specific (S) — single dominant design emerges. Once dominant design is established, competition shifts from product innovation to process innovation and cost reduction.

[2. Fluid Phase — Pre-iPhone (pre-2007) - 2 marks]

Mobile phone market 2000-2007: No clear design standard, multiple competing paradigms:

  • Nokia: Numeric keypad + feature phone UI (dominant in dumb phone era)
  • BlackBerry: QWERTY keyboard + email focus (business professionals)
  • Motorola: Flip phones (RAZR) — fashion/portability
  • Windows Mobile: Stylus + touch (early smartphones)
  • Symbian OS: Various Nokia designs

No consensus on what a "smartphone" should look like or how it should be controlled.

[3. Transitional Phase — iPhone Launch + Response (2007-2010) - 3 marks]

iPhone (January 2007) introduced: full touchscreen, no physical keyboard, capacitive multi-touch, app store ecosystem. User experience immediately preferred over alternatives.

Google's Android adopted same paradigm within 12 months — confirming touchscreen slab as emergent standard. Competitors recognized: Samsung, LG, HTC all transition to touchscreen designs. BlackBerry and Nokia cling to keyboards — fatal delay.

[4. Specific Phase — Touchscreen Slab as Dominant Design (2010+) - 1 mark]

By 2010: full-touchscreen, large-screen slab = universal smartphone form factor. Competition shifts to: camera quality, processor speed, software features, price points. Physical keyboard smartphones disappear from mainstream market. BlackBerry eliminated from consumer market by 2016.

[5. Strategic Implications - 1 mark]

Nokia & BlackBerry failure: Late adoption of dominant design = market share collapse. First-mover advantage of dominant design establishment: Apple locked in iPhone ecosystem, developer loyalty, App Store revenue before competitors could respond fully.

Common Confusion: S-Curve vs Rogers Diffusion of Innovation

S-Curve (Technology Performance)

Plots TECHNOLOGY PERFORMANCE vs. R&D investment or time. About how good the technology gets. Slow-fast-slow improvement pattern.

Rogers Diffusion (Adoption)

Plots ADOPTER CATEGORIES vs. time (Innovators → Early Adopters → Early Majority → Late Majority → Laggards). About who adopts and when.

Both produce S-shaped curves but measure different things. S-curve = technology capability. Rogers = market adoption behavior. Both can apply to same technology but from different angles.

Case Study Application: Innovation Theories

Identify which of the 4 SPAD theories applies to the case company/technology
For S-curve: identify which phase (Emergence/Growth/Maturity) and what evidence supports it
For Punctuated Eq: identify the equilibrium and the punctuation event
For Dominant Design: identify the fluid-transitional-specific phases and which design won
Connect to absorptive capacity: did the firm have prior knowledge to recognize and adapt?
