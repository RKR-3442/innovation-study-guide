CHAPTER 12

🌐 Innovation Offshoring

MEMORIZE: Definition + 4 Benefits

Definition

Relocating R&D and innovation activities to foreign countries

4 Benefits - "CAST"

  • Cost Efficiency (lower R&D costs)
  • Access to Specialized Skills (global talent)
  • Speed (24/7 development across time zones)
  • Target Markets (market expansion opportunities)
Global Innovation Networks (GINs)

Definition: Collaborations between firms/organizations globally to produce & exploit new knowledge

Key Point: NOT zero-sum - creates value for ALL participants

UNDERSTAND: Modularization Concept

Why offshoring works: Products can be broken into "decomposable building blocks" (modules) that different companies in different countries can develop independently.

Apple iPod Example: Sony/Toshiba (Japan) + Samsung (Korea) + Texas Instruments (USA) + Wolfson (Scotland) = All worked on different modules. Apple did final integration.

Critical Success Factor: Strong IP protection in host country (firms won't offshore R&D to countries with weak IP laws)

Mention "CAST" benefits, explain modularization with Apple example, note that Asia plays central role (India, China, South Korea, Singapore). Don't forget: GINs are win-win, not zero-sum!
CHAPTER 11

⚙️ Managing Innovation

MEMORIZE: 4 Management Functions (POLC)

Remember: "POLC" (Like "Police" without the 'ie')

  • Planning (project management, stage-gates)
  • Organizing (structure, teams)
  • Leading (vision, culture, empowerment)
  • Controlling (monitoring, corrective action)
Stage-Gate Process (3 Gates)
  • Gate 1: Initial Screening → Commit resources?
  • Gate 2: Business Analysis → Full-scale development?
  • Gate 3: Pre-Commercialization → Full production/launch?

Outcomes at each gate: GO / HOLD / KILL

4 Components of Innovation Management
C-S-S-C (like "CS:SC")
  • Capabilities (people, skills, money)
  • Structures (processes, systems)
  • Strategy (long-term plan)
  • Culture (risk-taking, learning from failure)
Google's 70-20-10 Rule
  • 70% resources → Core business
  • 20% resources → Adjacent opportunities
  • 10% resources → Transformational (risky) innovations

UNDERSTAND: Development Funnel Concept

Logic: Start with MANY ideas (wide mouth of funnel) → Filter systematically → End with FEW successful launches

Why? Most innovations fail, so you need many ideas to get one success. Filtering reduces wasted resources on bad ideas.

Funnel connects to Stage-Gates: Gates are the filtering points. At each gate, ideas are GO/HOLD/KILL based on: (1) Makes business sense? (2) Essential steps completed? (3) On time/budget?
Always mention "POLC" functions. Explain development funnel = many ideas→few launches. State 70-20-10 rule if asked about resource allocation. For culture, list: risk-taking, learning from failure, continuous learning, idea generation.
CHAPTER 10

💰 Funding Innovation

MEMORIZE: Funding Trajectory (5 Stages)

Think: "Pre→Seed→ABC" (like learning alphabet)

Stage Amount Source What You Need
Pre-Seed Small 3 Fs (Founder, Family, Friends) Just an idea
Seed £50K-£250K Angel Investors Prototype/MVP
Series A $2-15M Venture Capital Proven business model
Series B $10-50M+ Later-stage VCs Scale-up ready
Series C+ $25M+ Hedge funds, Banks Major growth/acquisition
Angel Investors vs. VCs
  • Angels: High net-worth individuals, invest in PEOPLE as much as ideas, expect capital gains (not dividends), take higher risks
  • VCs: Professional firms, need proven business model, larger amounts, act as intermediaries for institutional money
UAE Funding - Know These Numbers
  • MBR Innovation Fund: AED 2B (for registered IP)
  • Khalifa Fund: AED 2B (for SME development, up to AED 10M for industrial)
  • Commercial Banks: 40 of 52 banks offer SME services

UNDERSTAND: The Cash Flow Problem

Innovation Cashflow Problem = Negative cash flow + Uncertain breakeven + No track record = Can't borrow from banks

Why each stage exists:

  • Pre-Seed: Too risky for anyone except you and people who trust you
  • Seed: Angels willing to bet on people/potential
  • Series A: VCs need proven model (reduced risk)
  • Series B/C: Now it's about growth speed, not survival
Financial Bootstrapping = Getting resources at little/no cost (e.g., garage office, personal car for business). Steve Jobs started Apple in his parents' garage!
Draw the funding trajectory table. Remember "Pre→Seed→ABC". For UAE context, cite the two AED 2B funds. Explain why banks don't fund early innovation (cash flow problem). Mention Angels invest in people, VCs invest in proven models.

🎯 Innovation Strategy

MEMORIZE: 5 Key Components of Innovation Strategy

Remember: "VGRPC" (like "VGR PC" - Video Game Ready PC)

  • Vision (clear direction, inspires employees)
  • Goals (specific, measurable objectives)
  • Resources (funding, people, infrastructure)
  • Processes (idea generation → evaluation → implementation)
  • Culture (encourages risk-taking, experimentation)
Innovation Maturity Levels (5 Levels)
Level Name Quick Description
1 Ad-hoc Unplanned, rarely succeeds
2 Repeatable Some successes, inconsistent
3 Defined Structured processes company-wide
4 Predictable Data-driven with measurable ROI
5 Optimized Continuous improvement, embedded
ISO 56000 - 8 Principles (Just know 4 main ones)
  • 1. Realization of Value (create financial/social value)
  • 2. Future-focused Leaders (long-term over short-term)
  • 3. Strategic Direction (innovation aligned with goals)
  • 4. Culture (curiosity, risk-taking, collaboration)

UNDERSTAND: Strategic Alignment Concept

Key Principle: Innovation strategy MUST align with overall business strategy. Think of it as GPS - keeps innovation efforts on track toward company goals.

Apple Example: Business Strategy = User-centric design + integrated ecosystem. Innovation Strategy = All innovations (iPhone, iPad, Apple Watch) follow this principle. Everything works together seamlessly. Strategy guides WHICH innovations to pursue.

Why Strategy Matters:

  • Clarifies priorities (what to work on)
  • Fosters alignment (everyone works toward same goals)
  • Prevents complacency (keeps pushing forward)
  • Ensures long-term success (not just lucky one-time hits)
Always define innovation strategy first. List "VGRPC" components. Mention strategic alignment (innovation must match business goals). If asked about maturity, describe 5 levels (1=ad-hoc to 5=optimized). Use Apple or Google as example.
CHAPTER 7

⚖️ Intellectual Property Rights (IPR)

MEMORIZE: 4 Main Types of IPR

Remember: "PTCD" (like "PT CD" - Physical Training CD)

Type Duration Protects What? Example
Patents 20 years Inventions (novel, useful, non-obvious) Pharmaceutical drugs, tech processes
Trademarks 10 yrs (renewable forever) Brand names, logos, sounds, colors Nike swoosh, "Just Do It"
Copyright Life + 50-70 years Creative works (books, music, software) Harry Potter books, songs
Designs 10-25 years Aesthetic appearance of products iPhone shape, furniture design
Patent Requirements (3 Tests)
NUI (like "New-ee")
  • Novelty (must be new, not previously disclosed)
  • Useful / Industrial Application (can be made/used in industry)
  • Inventive Step (non-obvious to skilled person)
Copyright Key Point

Automatic Protection: Copyright springs to life when work is created (NO registration needed)

Idea-Expression Dichotomy: Protects EXPRESSION of ideas, NOT the ideas themselves

Example: You can't copyright "idea for soccer-themed restaurant" but you CAN copyright detailed written description of how it works

Famous Trademark Examples to Remember
  • Sound: Nokia ringtone
  • Color: Tiffany Blue (Pantone 1837), UPS Brown
  • Shape: Coca-Cola bottle, Chanel perfume bottle

UNDERSTAND: Why IPR Exists (The Trade-Off)

Temporary Monopoly → Incentivizes Innovation → Inventor Profits → Eventually Public Domain → Society Benefits

The Logic:

  • Without IPR: Anyone can copy your invention immediately → You can't recoup R&D costs → Nobody invests in innovation
  • With IPR: You get exclusive rights for limited time → Can profit from invention → After expiry, everyone benefits
IBM Example: Aggressive IP strategy increased patent royalties from $30M (1990) to $1B (2000). Shows IPR isn't just about blocking others - it's about MONETIZING innovation through licensing!

Commercialization Strategies: Direct exploitation (make/sell yourself), Licensing (others pay royalties), Cross-licensing (exchange rights), Sale/assignment (sell IP outright)

Always use "PTCD" table. For patents, mention "NUI" requirements. Explain copyright is automatic (no registration). State the trade-off: temporary monopoly incentivizes innovation, then public benefits. Give IBM or J.K. Rowling ($1B from Harry Potter) as success example.
CHAPTER 3

📊 Theories of Innovation

MEMORIZE: 4 Major Theories

Remember: "SPAD" (like "Spade" card)

  • S-Curve
  • Punctuated Equilibrium
  • Absorptive Capacity
  • Dominant Design
S-Curve: 3 Phases
Phase What Happens Why
Slow Start Modest improvements Technology poorly understood
Rapid Growth Exponential improvements Learning curve effect
Maturity Diminishing returns Approaching physical limits

Key Point: At inflection point (maturity), time to adopt NEW technology or be displaced!

Punctuated Equilibrium Pattern
Equilibrium → PUNCTURE → Ferment → New Equilibrium
  • Equilibrium: Long periods of incremental innovation
  • Puncture (Discontinuity): Radical breakthrough (often from outsiders)
  • Ferment: Experimentation period with high uncertainty
  • New Equilibrium: Stabilizes around new technology
Dominant Design: 3 Phases
F-T-S (like "FTS" or "Fits")
  • Fluid: Multiple competing designs, high experimentation
  • Transitional: Dominant design emerges
  • Specific: Standard established, only incremental improvements

Key Insight: Dominant designs are rarely technically superior - they win through usability, network effects, or market power

Absorptive Capacity: 3 Components
R-A-A (like "RAA" sound)
  • Recognition: Spot external opportunities
  • Assimilation: Diffuse knowledge throughout organization
  • Application: Transform knowledge into innovations
Kondratiev Long Waves - Know Current & Next
  • 5th Wave (1980-2030): Computers, Telecommunications, Internet
  • 6th Wave (2030+): Nanotechnology, Biotechnology, Robotics, AI

UNDERSTAND: When to Use Each Theory

S-Curve: Use when analyzing technology life cycles or deciding when to switch technologies

Example: Storage evolution - Floppy → CD → USB → Cloud. Each new technology started its own S-curve when previous reached maturity.

Punctuated Equilibrium: Use when explaining why incumbents fail and new entrants succeed

4 Causes of Inertia (why incumbents resist): Sunk costs, Traditions, Internal politics, Outmoded expertise

Dominant Design: Use when analyzing industry standards or explaining market convergence

Example: iPhone (2007) established touchscreen as dominant design. Before: multiple designs (keyboards, styluses, various OS). After: Everyone copies touchscreen format.

Absorptive Capacity: Use when explaining why exposure to knowledge ≠ innovation

Explains why some companies, despite seeing same trends as competitors, can't innovate. They lack capacity to absorb and apply external knowledge. Requires internal R&D, networking, organizational learning.

Schumpeter's Creative Destruction: Radical innovations cause economic reconfiguration - new industries arise, old ones decline. Change is the norm, not stability.

Use "SPAD" mnemonic. Draw S-curve showing 3 phases. Explain Punctuated Equilibrium with music example (Vinyl→Cassette→CD→MP3→Streaming). For Dominant Design, use "FTS" and iPhone example. For Absorptive Capacity, explain "RAA" and why companies fail to innovate despite exposure.

    CHAPTER 3

    📊 Theories of Innovation

    Purpose of Innovation Theories: Provide analytical frameworks to understand, explain, and predict innovation patterns. Help categorize innovations and identify success factors.

    Theory 1: Technology S-Curve

    Concept: Technology performance follows an S-shaped progression over time

    Three Phases:

    1. Slow Initial Progress (Learning Phase)

      Early investment produces modest improvements as technology is poorly understood

    2. Rapid Improvement (Growth Phase)

      Successive engineering efforts deliver exponentially greater performance gains. "Learning curve" effect - better understanding enables better quality at lower cost

    3. Diminishing Returns (Maturity Phase)

      Technology approaches physical limits. Increasing effort produces decreasing performance gains

    Storage Technology Evolution:
    Floppy disks → CDs → USB drives → External drives → Cloud storage

    Each transition occurred when: Previous technology reached maturity/physical limits, and new technology entered growth phase

    Key Implication: Watch for inflection point where existing technology enters decline. Time to adopt successor technology, or risk being displaced by competitors with new technology.

    Theory 2: Punctuated Equilibrium

    Concept: Long periods of incremental innovation (equilibrium) interrupted by radical breakthroughs (punctuation)

    Pattern of Change:

    Equilibrium Period:

    • Incremental innovations dominate
    • Existing firms focus on refining current technology
    • Stability and predictability

    ↓ Radical Innovation (Discontinuity) ↓

    • Major technological breakthrough
    • Often from new firms or industry outsiders
    • Renders old competencies obsolete

    Ferment Period:

    • Experimentation with new technology
    • Multiple competing designs
    • High uncertainty

    ↓ New Equilibrium ↓

    Why Incumbent Firms Resist Change (Causes of Inertia):

    • Sunk Costs: Previous investments tied to old technology
    • Traditions: "Way we've always done it" mentality
    • Internal Politics: Managers committed to old technology
    • Outmoded Expertise: Knowledge becomes irrelevant
    Music Storage Formats:
    Vinyl (1950s) → equilibrium → Cassette (1960s-70s) → equilibrium → CD (1982-1997) → equilibrium → MP3 (1997+) → equilibrium → Streaming (2010s+)

    Theory 3: Dominant Design

    Concept: After a period of competing designs, the market converges on a single standard that becomes the accepted norm

    Three Phases:

    Phase Characteristics Innovation Type
    Fluid Multiple competing designs, high experimentation, no clear winner Radical, exploratory
    Transitional Dominant design emerges, gaining significant recognition Focus on refining emerging standard
    Specific Widespread acceptance of single design standard Incremental improvements only

    Factors Creating Dominant Designs:

    • Consumer Preference: Design better meets user requirements
    • Market Power: Dominant producer establishes standard
    • Network Effects: Value increases with more users
    • Regulation: Government or industry mandates
    Smartphone Dominant Design:
    Before iPhone (2007): Multiple designs (physical keyboards, styluses, various OS)
    After iPhone: Touchscreen + app ecosystem became dominant design
    Now: All smartphones follow similar format with incremental improvements

    Important Note: Dominant designs are rarely technically superior - they win through usability, standards, and business strategy

    Theory 4: Absorptive Capacity

    Concept: A firm's ability to recognize, assimilate, and apply external knowledge for commercial purposes

    Three Components:

    1. Recognition

      Scanning external environment for technological opportunities and new knowledge. Requires "conduit" between external environment and organization

    2. Assimilation

      Knowledge diffusion throughout organization. Breaking down silos, ensuring ideas reach all levels and departments

    3. Application

      Transforming absorbed knowledge into commercial innovations. Requires complementary capabilities and resources

    Key Success Factors:

    • Internal R&D: Prior related knowledge enables recognition
    • Networking: External partnerships and collaborations
    • Organizational Learning: Systems for capturing and sharing knowledge
    • Balance: Between looking inward (internal capabilities) and outward (external opportunities)

    Why It Matters: Explains why some organizations, despite exposure to external knowledge, remain poor at innovation - they fail to effectively absorb and apply that knowledge

    Kondratiev Long Wave Cycles

    Economic cycles of ~50-60 years driven by technological innovations:

    Period Wave Leading Technologies
    1780-1830 First Cotton, iron, water power
    1830-1880 Second Railways, steam power, steamship
    1880-1930 Third Electricity, chemicals, steel
    1930-1980 Fourth Cars, electronics, oil, aerospace
    1980-2030 Fifth Computers, telecommunications, Internet
    2030+ Sixth Nanotechnology, biotechnology, robotics, AI?

    Schumpeter's Creative Destruction

    Creative Destruction: Radical innovations from within organizations (due to competition) cause dramatic economic reconfiguration - new industries arise, old industries decline.
    Examples of Creative Destruction:
    • Netflix/Streaming replaced Blockbuster/Video rental
    • Amazon forced shopping malls to adapt or close
    • Digital cameras destroyed film industry
    • Smartphones replaced cameras, GPS devices, music players
    • VOIP disrupted telephone industry
    These theories provide powerful analytical frameworks: S-curves predict technology transitions, Punctuated Equilibrium explains periods of stability vs. disruption, Dominant Design shows market convergence patterns, and Absorptive Capacity reveals why knowledge access ≠ innovation capability.
